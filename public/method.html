Our method

The questions come from published standards and research on how courts should be judged by the people who use them. Here is what we used, why, and what the result cannot tell you.

The rules we followed

  • Ask about what people can see (the process), not about legal correctness or the verdict.
  • One idea per question.
  • Plain language, at about an 8th-grade level.
  • Give an honest way out: “I didn’t see this”. Require at least three answers.
  • Ask whether the person won or lost, and show it, because losing colors how people rate.
  • Show groups of raters separately instead of blending them.

The five judge questions

Did the judge let each side be heard?

Measures “voice”. Based on the court-user survey of the National Center for State Courts (“I had a chance to tell my side of the story”), the Arizona juror survey, and research by Lind, Tyler and Huo on what makes people feel a court treated them fairly.

Did the judge treat both sides the same?

Measures neutrality, the best everyday stand-in for impartiality. Based on NCSC (“I was treated the same as everyone else”) and the Arizona survey.

Was the judge respectful to everyone?

Measures respect and temperament. Based on NCSC (“courtesy and respect”), the Arizona survey, and the temperament criterion of the ABA guidelines.

Was it clear what the judge decided, and why?

Measures understanding. Based on NCSC (“I could follow what was happening”, “I know what to do next”) and the communication criterion of the ABA guidelines.

Did the judge run things on time?

Measures time and organization. Based on NCSC (“a reasonable amount of time”), the Arizona survey, and the case-management category used by Colorado’s own evaluations. In Rhode Island’s 2023 survey, time was one of the lowest-scoring areas.

The five prosecutor questions

No official questionnaire for prosecutors exists. These are a careful first draft built from the ABA prosecution standards, the same procedural-justice research, and the Stanford “Rate My District Attorney” report.

Was the prosecutor respectful to everyone in the courtroom?

Respect. Same research as above.

Did the prosecutor seem prepared?

The duty to prepare. Visible in court.

Did the prosecutor stick to the facts?

Candor and the standard against inflammatory argument. It asks about conduct a person can see, not whether the facts were true.

Could you follow what the prosecutor was saying and doing?

Understanding, one of the elements of procedural justice.

Did the prosecutor treat witnesses and the defendant fairly?

Fair conduct toward people in the room. We ask about behavior, not about motives.

Only for victims and witnesses (optional): Did the prosecutor listen to you and keep you informed? Did you feel safe and treated with dignity?

Based on research on victims’ and witnesses’ experience, where being informed and listened to matters most. Shown separately and never merged into the main score.

What we deliberately do not ask

  • Whether a verdict, sentence or plea was fair, or whether a judge got the law right. People in the room cannot judge that, and answers would mostly reflect who won.
  • Whether someone is “good”, “honest” or “corrupt”. We ask about things people did that others can see.
  • About charging decisions, plea deals or evidence disclosure. These happen where the public cannot see them.
  • About looks, politics or personal life.

Where the questions come from

  • ABA Black Letter Guidelines for the Evaluation of Judicial Performance (summary by IAALS)
  • Rhode Island Judiciary, 2023 Access and Fairness Survey (uses the NCSC CourTools Measure 1 items)
  • Arizona Supreme Court Administrative Order 2010-22 (juror and litigant evaluation questions)
  • ABA Criminal Justice Standards for the Prosecution Function
  • Prosecutorial Performance Indicators project
  • Stanford Law School, “Rate My District Attorney” (2018)

Also: Lind, Tyler and Huo (1997) and Tyler and Huo, “Trust in the Law”, on how people judge courts by fair treatment more than by outcome.

The appeals record on judge profiles

  • Source: the Colorado Court of Appeals’ own weekly announcements of its decisions, from June 2023. Each entry opens the announcement it came from, on the right page.
  • We keep our own copy of every announcement we link to. Next to each entry, “our saved copy” opens it, so it can still be checked if the court’s file is moved or removed. The official link always comes first.
  • A decision is listed under a judge only when the announcement names that judge as the trial judge and the name matches exactly one judge on our list in that court district (or the same full name, once in the whole state, for judges who changed courts). Magistrates are not included.
  • The outcome uses the court’s own wording. “Partly affirmed” means part of the ruling was reversed or vacated and the rest stands. When the court only declined to hear part of an appeal, that is not counted against the judge.
  • We do not name the people in the case, only the type of case.
  • We show the list, not a reversal rate: there is no public count of every case a judge decides, and some kinds of cases are appealed far more often than others.
  • A denied petition, including a denied C.A.R. 21 petition, is not an endorsement of the lower court’s ruling. We list decisions only, and never count a denial in a judge’s favor.
  • Updated every week. If you see a mistake, tell us on the Corrections page.

Judicial discipline in Colorado, statewide

The Colorado Commission on Judicial Discipline reviews complaints about judges (it calls them Requests for Evaluation). Its annual reports give statewide numbers only, never per judge, so these figures say nothing about any single judge on this site.

  • 2024: 368 requests received. 318 were dismissed at the first review. The Commission considered 65 matters and dismissed 29 of them. 3 formal proceedings, each ending in public censure. 12 private disciplinary actions, all for missed financial-disclosure filings. 2 diversion plans and 1 temporary suspension.
  • 2023: 344 requests received. 251 were dismissed at the first review.
  • Most complaints never become public. Since Amendment H (2024), a case becomes public when the Commission starts formal proceedings; informal proceedings, where most complaints end, stay confidential (Colorado Constitution, art. VI, § 23(3)(g)).

How the state’s surveys are collected

For comparison, this is how the official surveys work, taken from the state’s own 2024 survey reports.

  • They are conducted by a private research firm outside Colorado. The reports name it on the cover: Market Decisions Research, 511 Congress Street, Portland, Maine.
  • Invitations go out by email, in quarterly batches with email reminders: to attorneys, to other people who appeared in court, and to court staff. Jurors are also invited by email, and anyone can answer through a public link.
  • The invitation can come months after the hearing: attorneys who appeared in court from January to June 2023 were first emailed on September 10, 2023.
  • The reports count invitations sent, not invitations delivered. Whether an email reached an inbox, landed in a spam folder or went to an old address is not reported.
  • 119,479 email invitations to attorneys produced 5,682 evaluations. 117,355 people other than attorneys were invited by email, and 6,614 of them responded (6%).

Three real tables, cropped from individual judges’ reports, are on the About page.

The limits, honestly

  • These questions are drafted from standards and research. They have not yet been tested with real users, so we do not claim they are a validated scientific instrument.
  • People who choose to rate are not a random sample of everyone who goes to court.
  • A score summarizes opinions about conduct in a courtroom. It is not a finding about a judge’s legal skill.
  • We could confirm some of our sources only through summaries. We will replace them with the primary documents as we read them.

How we will check and improve them (planned)

  • A pilot with real courtroom visitors in Colorado.
  • Checking that people understand each question the same way and that the answers hang together.
  • Review by independent judicial-performance researchers, survey methodologists and practicing Colorado attorneys.
  • Review of the Spanish by native speakers.
  • Publishing every change to a question and the reason.
